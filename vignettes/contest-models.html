Contest models: from the multinomial to competitive breeding values

This vignette derives, without skipped steps, the two estimators BreedingR uses for competitive ability measured through grouped contests: the coupled Laplace estimator of the genetic variance of contest strength, and the composite-likelihood test for a rival-dependent (differential) indirect effect. Every transition is justified: which identity is used, and why one line follows from the previous one. It is pure method — no data enter it. The worked object throughout is a group contest: several competitors share a group, a fixed number of discrete outcomes is divided among them, and we observe the division.

1. The contest likelihood

A group \(g\) holds competitors \(i \in \mathcal{P}_g\). Competitor \(i\) enters with a known exposure \(s_{i}>0\) (the opportunity bought — a dose, a number of trials) and carries a latent strength \(\lambda_i>0\). The \(N_g\) outcomes of the group are divided by a biased lottery: each outcome falls to \(i\) with probability

\[p_{ig} \;=\; \frac{s_{i}\,\lambda_i}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{P}_g} s_{k}\,\lambda_k}, \qquad (y_{1g},\dots,y_{kg}) \;\sim\; \mathrm{Multinomial}\!\left(N_g;\, p_{1g},\dots,p_{kg}\right).\]

Write \(\eta_i=\log\lambda_i\) and take the exposure as an offset \(o_i=\log s_i\). With two competitors this is the Bradley–Terry model [@bt1952]; with more it is Plackett–Luce [@luce1959; @plackett1975], equivalently the conditional logit. The strengths are defined only up to a common scale: multiplying every \(\lambda\) in a group by the same constant leaves every \(p_{ig}\) unchanged, because the constant factors out of numerator and denominator. Only differences of \(\eta\) are identifiable; an anchor is required (a zero-mean random term supplies one).

2. Reduction to a pair, by exact conditioning

Take a pair \(\{i,j\}\subseteq\mathcal{P}_g\) and condition on their joint count \(m=y_{ig}+y_{jg}\). The claim is that the conditional distribution of \(y_{ig}\) is Binomial and depends on no other competitor. Start from the multinomial mass function and hold every count except \(y_{ig},y_{jg}\) fixed:

\[\Pr\!\left(y_{ig}=a,\; y_{jg}=m-a \mid \{y_{kg}\}_{k\neq i,j}\right) \;=\; \frac{\dfrac{N_g!}{a!\,(m-a)!\,\prod_{k\neq i,j} y_{kg}!}\; p_{ig}^{\,a}\,p_{jg}^{\,m-a}\,\prod_{k\neq i,j} p_{kg}^{\,y_{kg}}} {\displaystyle\sum_{a'=0}^{m}\dfrac{N_g!}{a'!\,(m-a')!\,\prod_{k\neq i,j} y_{kg}!}\; p_{ig}^{\,a'}\,p_{jg}^{\,m-a'}\,\prod_{k\neq i,j} p_{kg}^{\,y_{kg}}}.\]

Every factor that does not involve \(a\) — the \(N_g!\), all the \(\prod_{k\neq i,j}\) terms, the fixed other counts — is identical in the numerator and in every summand of the denominator, so it cancels:

\[\Pr\!\left(y_{ig}=a \mid y_{ig}+y_{jg}=m\right) \;=\; \frac{\dbinom{m}{a}\,p_{ig}^{\,a}\,p_{jg}^{\,m-a}} {\displaystyle\sum_{a'=0}^{m}\dbinom{m}{a'}\,p_{ig}^{\,a'}\,p_{jg}^{\,m-a'}}.\]

The denominator is a binomial expansion, \(\sum_{a'=0}^{m}\binom{m}{a'}p_{ig}^{\,a'}p_{jg}^{\,m-a'}=(p_{ig}+p_{jg})^{m}\), so

\[y_{ig}\mid(y_{ig}+y_{jg}=m)\;\sim\;\mathrm{Binomial}(m,\pi_{ij}), \qquad \pi_{ij}=\frac{p_{ig}}{p_{ig}+p_{jg}}=\frac{s_i\lambda_i}{s_i\lambda_i+s_j\lambda_j}.\]

This is exact, not an approximation: conditioning on the pair total removes the rest of the group from the problem, because the other competitors’ probabilities never survive the cancellation. Taking the log-odds,

\[\mathrm{logit}\,\pi_{ij} =\log\frac{p_{ig}}{p_{jg}} =(o_i-o_j)+(\eta_i-\eta_j). \tag{2.1}\]

The reduction to pairs is not a convenience: Section 3 shows it is the only place where a rival-dependent effect is identifiable.

3. What is estimable — the aliasing of a uniform indirect effect

Give each competitor a direct log-strength \(a_i\) and an indirect effect it exerts on its groupmates. Write the realized log-strength of \(i\) in group \(g\) as its own ability plus the effects the others exert on it:

\[\eta_{ig}=a_i+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_g,\,j\neq i} c_{j\to i},\]

where \(c_{j\to i}\) is the effect of \(j\) on \(i\). Two cases.

Uniform indirect effect — \(j\) acts on every groupmate alike, \(c_{j\to i}=c_j\). Then

\[\eta_{ig}=a_i+\sum_{j\neq i}c_j =a_i+\Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{P}_g}c_j}_{\;=\,C_g,\ \text{constant in }i}\Big)-c_i =(a_i-c_i)+C_g.\]

Substituting into the choice probability, the group-constant \(C_g\) leaves through the same door the scale did in Section 1:

\[p_{ig}=\frac{s_i\,e^{\,(a_i-c_i)+C_g}}{\sum_{k} s_k\,e^{\,(a_k-c_k)+C_g}} =\frac{s_i\,e^{\,a_i-c_i}}{\sum_{k} s_k\,e^{\,a_k-c_k}},\]

because \(e^{C_g}\) multiplies numerator and denominator and cancels. Only the combination \(b_i=a_i-c_i\) enters. Hence the reparametrization

\[(a_i,\,c_i)\;\longmapsto\;(a_i+t,\;c_i+t)\quad\text{for any constant }t\]

leaves \(b_i\), and therefore every probability and the whole likelihood, exactly unchanged. Direct and uniform-indirect effects are perfectly aliased: no estimator separates them, at any sample size. This is a property of the design, not of the software. (On the share scale — outcomes as proportions summing to one — the same collinearity appears as a direct–indirect correlation pinned at \(-1\), where the gradient does not vanish and a delta-method standard error is meaningless.)

Differential (rival-dependent) indirect effect — \(j\) acts differently depending on who the rival is, e.g. by the rival’s lineage. Model the departure from uniform as

\[c_{j\to i}=c_j+\delta_j\,\mathbb{1}[\,\text{class}(i)\neq\text{class}(j)\,].\]

Now form the pair contrast (2.1). The own-abilities and the uniform parts enter \(\eta_i-\eta_j\); the group-constant \(C_g\) and any term common to \(i\) and \(j\) cancel in the difference, but the class-specific term does not, because it is asymmetric in \(i\) and \(j\):

\[\mathrm{logit}\,\pi_{ij} =(o_i-o_j)+(\eta_i-\eta_j) =(o_i-o_j)+(a_i-a_j) +\delta_i\,\mathbb{1}[\text{class}(j)\neq\text{class}(i)] -\delta_j\,\mathbb{1}[\text{class}(i)\neq\text{class}(j)]. \tag{3.1}\]

The pair is exactly where the differential effect becomes visible: a uniform shift adds the same amount to \(\eta_i\) and \(\eta_j\) and dies in the difference, while a rival-dependent shift survives. The estimable quantity \(\delta_i\) is the change in \(i\)’s log-odds of winning an outcome from \(j\) when \(j\) is of a different class, over and above the two competitors’ abilities.

4. Composite likelihood, and what it costs

Break every group into its pairs and multiply the pair binomials as if independent:

\[c\ell(\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{\delta}) =\sum_{\text{pairs }(i,j)}\log\mathrm{Binom}\!\left(y_{ig}\,;\,m_{ij},\,\pi_{ij}\right). \tag{4.1}\]

The pairs are not independent: a competitor appears in many of them, and within a group the counts are constrained to sum to \(N_g\). So (4.1) is a composite likelihood [@lindsay1988; @varin2011], not a likelihood. What survives that, and what breaks, is precise.

Consistency survives. Each factor is a genuine conditional probability (Section 2), so each per-pair score has mean zero at the true parameter: \(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla\log\mathrm{Binom}_p\right]=0\). The composite score is a sum of these,

\[\nabla c\ell=\sum_p \nabla\log\mathrm{Binom}_p, \qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla c\ell\right]=\sum_p \mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla\log\mathrm{Binom}_p\right]=0,\]

so the estimating equation \(\nabla c\ell=0\) is unbiased and its root is consistent under the usual regularity conditions. Unbiasedness of the score needs the marginals to be correct, not the pairs to be independent — which is exactly why the composite construction is legitimate here.

The second Bartlett identity breaks. For a true likelihood, the expected negative Hessian equals the variance of the score, \(H\equiv-\mathbb{E}[\nabla^2\ell]=\operatorname{Var}(\nabla\ell)\equiv J\). For the composite likelihood the two objects come apart:

\[H=-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla^2 c\ell\right]=\sum_p -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla^2\log\mathrm{Binom}_p\right],\]

a sum of per-pair informations, whereas

\[J=\operatorname{Var}\!\left(\nabla c\ell\right) =\sum_p\sum_q \operatorname{Cov}\!\left(\nabla\log\mathrm{Binom}_p,\;\nabla\log\mathrm{Binom}_q\right).\]

The off-diagonal \(p\neq q\) terms are nonzero precisely because pairs sharing a competitor are dependent, so \(J\neq H\). Two consequences, both with teeth:

  1. The curvature is not the precision. The variance of \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) is the Godambe (sandwich) matrix \(G^{-1}=H^{-1}JH^{-1}\) [@godambe1960], not \(H^{-1}\). A standard error read off the composite Hessian is invalid.

  2. The likelihood-ratio statistic is not \(\chi^2\). For a true likelihood \(2[\ell(\hat\theta)-\ell(\theta_0)]\to\chi^2_q\). For a composite likelihood the same Taylor expansion gives, instead [@kent1982],

    \[2\!\left[c\ell(\hat\theta)-c\ell(\theta_0)\right]\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\sum_{k=1}^{q}\lambda_k Z_k^2, \qquad Z_k\stackrel{iid}{\sim}\mathcal N(0,1),\]

    where \(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_q\) are the eigenvalues of \(H^{-1}J\). When \(H=J\) (a true likelihood) every \(\lambda_k=1\) and the mixture collapses to \(\chi^2_q\). Here the \(\lambda_k\) are unknown, so the reference distribution is unknown — and reading the statistic against a \(\chi^2\) table would invent it.

5. Calibration by permutation

Because the \(\lambda_k\) are unknown — and because real overdispersion (a batch or handling effect shared within a group) inflates \(J\) beyond what the multinomial predicts — the null distribution of the statistic is obtained empirically. The null must reproduce everything that shapes the statistic — the dependence among pairs, the overdispersion, the group sizes, the exposures — and break only the association under test, between the rival’s class and the outcome. A permutation that relabels which groups are class-mixed, among groups of the same size, with the counts and exposures untouched, does exactly this: it severs class from outcome while preserving the dependence structure and the dispersion. The empirical \(p\)-value is

\[p=\frac{1+\#\{\,\text{permutation statistic}\ \geq\ \text{observed}\,\}}{1+B},\]

whose floor is \(1/(1+B)\) for \(B\) permutations. A parametric bootstrap that samples from the fitted multinomial is not a valid null here: it assumes the multinomial is the truth and so omits the overdispersion, making the null too tight; it is kept only as a diagnostic — the gap between the permutation null and the parametric null measures the overdispersion the multinomial cannot hold.

6. The coupled variance components, by Laplace

The differential test estimates \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) as location parameters. The companion question — how much of contest strength is heritable — puts a pedigree-structured random effect on the strengths and asks for its variance. With \(\eta_i=a_i+q_i\), \(\mathbf{a}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_a\mathbf A)\) (additive, tied by the pedigree) and \(\mathbf{q}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_q\mathbf I)\) (a permanent competitor effect), the variance is a marginal likelihood over the random effects,

\[L(\sigma^2_a,\sigma^2_q)=\int \Big[\textstyle\prod_g \mathrm{Multinomial}\big(\mathbf y_g\mid \mathbf p(\mathbf u)\big)\Big]\, \mathcal N(\mathbf a\mid\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_a\mathbf A)\,\mathcal N(\mathbf q\mid\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_q\mathbf I)\;d\mathbf u, \qquad \mathbf u=(\mathbf a,\mathbf q).\]

There is no closed form: the integral is in the dimension of the pedigree. This is why “running REML” is not available — REML is the Gaussian case, where the integral of a normal against a normal is normal and closes. Here it must be approximated, and the approximation is a declared choice.

Laplace’s method. Write the integrand as \(e^{-g(\mathbf u)}\) with

\[g(\mathbf u)=-\ell(\mathbf u)+\tfrac12\,\mathbf a'\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf a/\sigma^2_a+\tfrac12\,\mathbf q'\mathbf q/\sigma^2_q,\]

where \(\ell\) is the multinomial log-likelihood. Expand \(g\) to second order about its minimizer \(\hat{\mathbf u}\) (the joint mode). The gradient vanishes there by definition of the mode, \(\nabla g(\hat{\mathbf u})=\mathbf 0\), so the first-order term of the Taylor expansion is exactly zero — this is the single fact that makes the method work:

\[g(\mathbf u)\approx g(\hat{\mathbf u})+\tfrac12(\mathbf u-\hat{\mathbf u})'\mathbf H(\mathbf u-\hat{\mathbf u}), \qquad \mathbf H=\nabla^2 g(\hat{\mathbf u})=\mathbf W+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf A^{-1}/\sigma^2_a & \\ & \mathbf I/\sigma^2_q\end{pmatrix},\]

with \(\mathbf W\) the multinomial information (below). The remaining integral is Gaussian and closes, \(\int e^{-\frac12(\mathbf u-\hat{\mathbf u})'\mathbf H(\mathbf u-\hat{\mathbf u})}d\mathbf u=(2\pi)^{n/2}|\mathbf H|^{-1/2}\), giving

\[-2\log L \;\approx\; \underbrace{-2\ell(\hat{\mathbf u})}_{\text{fit at the mode}} +\underbrace{\mathbf a'\mathbf A^{-1}\mathbf a/\sigma^2_a+\mathbf q'\mathbf q/\sigma^2_q}_{\text{pedigree and permanent penalties}} +\underbrace{n_a\log\sigma^2_a-\log|\mathbf A^{-1}|+n_q\log\sigma^2_q}_{\text{prior normalizers}} +\underbrace{\log|\mathbf H|}_{\text{cost of estimating }\hat{\mathbf u}}. \tag{6.1}\]

The multinomial information \(\mathbf W\). From \(\ell\), with \(\eta\) on the log scale,

\[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial\eta_i}=\sum_g\big(y_{ig}-N_g p_{ig}\big), \qquad -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial\eta_i\,\partial\eta_j}=\sum_g N_g\big(\delta_{ij}\,p_{ig}-p_{ig}p_{jg}\big),\]

so each group contributes the block \(N_g\big(\mathrm{diag}(\mathbf p_g)-\mathbf p_g\mathbf p_g'\big)\). Two facts follow. First, \(\mathbf W\) is sparse: entry \((i,j)\) is nonzero only if \(i\) and \(j\) shared a group. Sparsity comes from the design, and it is what makes the method affordable — \(\mathbf H=\mathbf W+(\text{block-diagonal precision})\) is sparse, and a single sparse Cholesky factorization delivers both the Newton step for the mode and the \(\log|\mathbf H|\) that (6.1) needs. Second, \(\mathbf W\) is a weighted graph Laplacian: its rows sum to zero (\(\sum_j N_g(\delta_{ij}p_{ig}-p_{ig}p_{jg})=N_g(p_{ig}-p_{ig})=0\)), so \(\mathbf W\) is singular on its own; the precision term \(\mathbf A^{-1}/\sigma^2_a\) is what makes \(\mathbf H\) positive definite.

What \(\log|\mathbf H|\) is, and is not. It is the price of having estimated the mode rather than known it; without it \(\sigma^2\) comes out systematically underestimated. It is not the REML penalty. Writing the Gaussian restricted likelihood in the standard form,

\[-2\log L_{\text{REML}}=(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)'\mathbf V^{-1}(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\mathbf b)+\log|\mathbf V|+\log|\mathbf X'\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf X|,\]

the Laplace term corresponds to \(\log|\mathbf V|\) (through the identity \(\log|\mathbf V|=\log|\mathbf R|+\log|\mathbf G|+\log|\mathbf H|\)), which is the maximum-likelihood term. The genuinely restricted term is \(\log|\mathbf X'\mathbf V^{-1}\mathbf X|\), which penalizes the fixed effects for having been estimated — and (6.1) does not carry it. So the estimator is marginal (ML) over the random effects, not REML. Calling it “REML–Laplace” is a common abuse; the honest statement is that REML would additionally integrate the fixed effects, which the same expansion could do. When the design has an offset and no free fixed effect — as the contest model does, exposure entering as an offset — the distinction does not bite numerically, but it does the moment a fixed effect enters.

The variance components are then estimated by minimizing (6.1) over \((\sigma^2_a,\sigma^2_q)\), re-finding the mode at each trial value. Because the surface of an additive/permanent model has a ridge — trading \(\sigma^2_a\) for \(\sigma^2_q\) at fixed sum barely moves the fit — a point estimate is not enough: the interval on the heritable fraction \(\sigma^2_a/(\sigma^2_a+\sigma^2_q)\) is read from a profile of (6.1) along that fraction, which is valid here because the multinomial is a true likelihood and the profile-likelihood ratio keeps its asymptotic calibration. (This is the one place the composite likelihood of Section 4 and the true likelihood here part ways: there, \(H\neq J\) forbids the \(\chi^2\) calibration; here it holds.)

7. Heritability of a contest-derived effect

Suppose the differential effects \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) of Section 3–5 are shown to exist and one asks whether they are heritable. Two routes, and the honest arithmetic of each.

Two-step, and its attenuation. Treat the estimates as a measurement-error model,

\[\hat\delta_i=\delta_i+e_i,\qquad \boldsymbol{\delta}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2_\delta\mathbf A),\qquad \operatorname{Var}(e_i)=v_i,\]

where \(v_i\) is the sampling variance of the composite estimate. Fitting an animal model to \(\hat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\) aims \(\sigma^2_\delta\) at the additive variance and loads \(\sigma^2_{\text{env}}+\bar v\) onto the residual, so the estimated heritability is

\[\mathbb{E}\!\left[\hat h^2_\delta\right]\approx \frac{\sigma^2_\delta}{\sigma^2_\delta+\sigma^2_{\text{env}}+\bar v} \;<\; \frac{\sigma^2_\delta}{\sigma^2_\delta+\sigma^2_{\text{env}}} =h^2_\delta,\]

biased downward by the sampling noise \(\bar v\) — a single direction, which makes a null result conservative but not conclusive: without the \(v_i\) it cannot separate “not heritable” from “a moderate \(\sigma^2_\delta\) drowned in noise”. Closing it needs the \(v_i\), from the Godambe sandwich or a pair bootstrap, entered as weights — with the caution that a weight built from the composite curvature alone (ignoring \(H\neq J\)) is optimistic and inflates the genetic fraction.

Coupled. Put \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) inside the composite likelihood with the pedigree prior and integrate by the Laplace machinery of Section 6 (the Hessian in \(\boldsymbol{\delta}\) is sparse for the same reason: \(\delta_i\) meets \(\delta_j\) only if they shared a class-mixed group). The one difference from Section 6 is that the base object is a composite likelihood, not a true one, so even the profile of its marginal does not inherit automatic \(\chi^2\) calibration — the interval on \(\sigma^2_\delta\) must come from simulation with a known truth on the real design, the validation pattern this whole family of estimators uses.

How the package exposes this

# strength per unit of exposure (Section 1-3), free of the sum-to-N constraint
bt <- competition_strength(wins, group, competitor, exposure = dose)

# the strength as a phenotype for an animal model, carrying its own precision
d2  <- data.frame(id = names(bt$strength), y = bt$log_strength, w = 1 / bt$se^2)
model(y ~ animal(id), d2, pedigree, weights = "w")

competition_strength() fits the strengths by the MM algorithm of @hunter2004 — which is the EM-MAP of @caron2012 with a Gamma prior, containing Zermelo’s [-@zermelo1929] iteration as the flat-prior case — and flags competitors outside the strongly connected component of the contest graph, where the estimate is prior shrinkage rather than measurement [@ford1957]. The coupled variance-component estimator of Section 6 and the differential test of Sections 4–5 are analysis-level constructions built on the same sparse pieces the package exposes (a_inverse(), sparse_chol(), sparse_solve(), selected_inverse()).
